Executive Overview

TPOS is a multi-tenant vertical SaaS that runs an independent trailer dealership’s entire operation — its public storefront and its whole back office — on one shared record of truth, where a single trailer is seen through 13 role lenses (inventory → deal or rental → work order → invoice → title → delivery → listing). Sold hosted (monthly), licensed (annual), or self-hosted (one-time).

The product

  • Who: small-to-mid used/refurbished commercial-trailer dealers, replacing a website builder + CRM + spreadsheets + accounting + listing sites with one role-aware platform.
  • Core loops: sell — acquire → recondition → price → list → lead → quote → finance → title → deliver → book; rent — reserve → check-out → on-rent (cycle billing) → return → inspect → available again. Each stage hands the same record to the next role.
  • Pricing: Hosted $199–$1,290/mo · annual license · one-time self-host.

Key qualities (§12)

  • Strict tenant isolation — no path crosses tenants.
  • Real-time — others’ changes appear without refresh or polling delay.
  • Responsive — good UX from phone to ultra-wide desktop.
  • Auditable by default; deploys hosted or self-host from one product.

1 · Product Vision & Business Model

TPOS is a vertical SaaS that gives an independent trailer dealership one system to run both its public storefront and its entire back office — acquisition, reconditioning, pricing, marketing, sales, rentals, financing, titling, delivery and bookkeeping — on a single shared record of truth.

The market is fragmented small-to-mid dealers (used & refurbished commercial trailers: dry vans, flatbeds, reefers, lowboys, dumps, tankers, chassis) who today stitch together a website builder, a generic CRM, spreadsheets, QuickBooks and listing sites (TruckPaper, Facebook). TPOS replaces that stack with a purpose-built, role-aware platform.

The platform operator (TPOS, Inc.) additionally runs a super-admin control plane to provision tenants, meter usage, bill subscriptions, and support dealers (see §6.13).

Product principles

  • One record, many lenses. A trailer is simultaneously inventory, a deal or a rental agreement, a work order, an invoice line, a title file, a delivery, and a public listing. Every role edits the same entity through a view shaped to its job — never a copy.
  • The storefront is generated, not separate. Inventory marked available is automatically published to the dealer's branded public site and syndicated to listing channels.
  • Role-first, not module-first. Each user lands on a workspace built around their daily goals and decisions, not a generic menu of tables.
  • Auditable by default. Price changes, discounts, status transitions and money movement are logged with who/when/why.

The status machine

An Inventory Unit moves through the sale path incoming → recon → available → pending → sold, and — when flagged into the rental pool — the rental loop available → reserved → on-rent → (overdue) → in-inspection → available. Status is the single field that re-shapes the unit across roles — e.g. available publishes it to the storefront; pending hides it from new leads; sold moves it to delivery & books revenue; on-rent starts cycle billing. Two timestamps matter at rental end: off-rent (billing stops) precedes returned (liability transfers). A rental unit can be retired to retail at any point — the same record simply re-enters the sale path with its cost basis and history intact. The system must guard valid transitions and log each one.

7 · Public Storefront

Every tenant gets a branded, mobile-first public website auto-generated from its available inventory. It is the demand engine that feeds the Lead Desk.

Functional requirements

  • Inventory grid with filter (type, condition, status), keyword search and sort (featured / price / year); each unit shows photos, key specs, price, location and a status badge.
  • Deep-linkable, shareable detail pages per unit (full specs, photos, description) with status (Available / Pending / Sold).
  • Lead capture: "Request a quote" and "Sell your trailer" forms create a Lead in the tenant's back office and notify staff; financing pre-qualification entry point.
  • Informational pages (financing, about, contact) and tenant branding (logo, colors, phone, address).
  • Units automatically appear/disappear as their status changes; optional syndication to external listing channels.
  • Rentals (when the dealer runs a fleet): a Rent section listing rental units with daily/weekly/monthly rates and availability; a “Request a rental” form creates a rental lead. Sale and rental listings draw from the same inventory records.
Public storefront — generated from available inventory; forms create leads in the back office.

Multi-role users

  • Combination is union (most-permissive-wins). A user's effective permissions are the union of their roles' permissions — e.g. a {sales, sales_manager} user both works their own deals (◑) and approves discounts (✓). Lot scope intersects independently: roles say what, lot scope says where.
  • Separation-of-duties exception. Union never lets one person be both sides of an approval: a user may not approve their own request (below-floor discount, price change, deposit application) even if their role set grants both capabilities. The system must route such approvals to a different qualified user — and if none exists in the tenant, surface that explicitly rather than silently self-approve. Each such case is audited.
  • Landing & switching. Each user has a primary role that decides their landing workspace; a visible workspace switcher exposes the others. Notifications and tasks aggregate across all held roles regardless of the active workspace.

13 · Acceptance Criteria

The rebuild is "done" when the following end-to-end scenarios pass for a tenant, with figures reconciling across roles:

  1. Acquire→list. Inventory Manager adds an incoming unit; Service completes intake & recon; Pricing sets list/floor; status flips to available and the unit appears on the storefront and syndication.
  2. Lead→quote. A storefront "Request a quote" creates a Lead in the Lead Desk within seconds, routes to a salesperson, who builds a Quote tied to the unit.
  3. Negotiate→approve. A below-floor offer requires Sales Manager approval, which is logged; the Deal advances to financing.
  4. Finance→title→deliver. Customer applies for financing in the portal and sees status; Office tracks title to "in hand"; Dispatch schedules and tracks delivery to "delivered."
  5. Book. Bookkeeper issues the invoice, records payment, and the sale appears in revenue/gross KPIs that match the Owner dashboard.
  6. Reserve→rent→return. Rental Manager reserves an available rental-pool unit; check-out is blocked until the customer has credit approval and a current certificate of insurance (or elects the damage waiver); a check-out inspection (photos + meter reading) is captured; the unit goes on-rent and a cycle invoice is generated; the customer calls off-rent (billing stops) and returns the unit (liability transfers); a check-in inspection assesses damage, the deposit is applied/released with an itemized statement, and the unit re-enters available; utilization KPIs update and rental revenue books as its own stream.
  7. Operate (hosted). Super-Admin provisions a new tenant, sees it billed, and watches MRR/usage update.

Appendix B · Security, Privacy & Compliance

A trailer dealer that arranges or extends financing is a “financial institution” under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act — which pulls the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314) onto every tenant, and onto TPOS as their service provider. These are binding legal requirements, not optional hardening. This appendix maps the regime to platform controls and a threat model.

Not legal advice. Items marked COUNSEL turn on facts a lawyer must confirm (whether a given dealer’s financing model triggers GLBA, FTC-vs-CFPB privacy applicability, per-state titling/lien law, and contractual liability allocation). Figures and citations are accurate as researched; verify against primary text before relying on them.

Liability. Primary duty is the dealer’s, and using a provider does not relieve it (§314.4(f)). But the FTC has acted directly against auto-dealer software providers (2019 settlement), so TPOS carries real exposure and allocates it by contract (MSA/DPA). COUNSEL

B.3 Privacy, e-signature & breach response

  • Privacy Rule. The dealer issues the notice + opt-out; TPOS generates/delivers notices and tracks opt-outs. The §313.13 service-provider exception (a contractual use-restriction) is what lets the dealer share customer NPI with TPOS without an opt-out — so that clause must be in the contract. Note: privacy rulemaking splits FTC (vehicle dealers) vs CFPB Reg P (others) — cite FTC 16 CFR 313 for dealers. COUNSEL
  • E-signature (ESIGN/UETA). Capture intent-to-sign; run the consumer-consent gate (affirmative consent + demonstrated ability to access the electronic form + the required pre-consent disclosures + a withdrawal mechanism); store tamper-evident, reproducible records (signer identity, timestamp, IP, document hash). Title documents are carved out — UETA §3 and many state codes exclude documents of title; treat titling as per-DMV, not generic e-sign.
  • Breach response. Instrument detection to count affected consumers and whether data was encrypted; auto-start the ≤ 30-day FTC clock (16 CFR 314.5) and fan out state notifications by the affected resident’s state. Capture residency in the data model so the per-state affected set is queryable — the same per-jurisdiction pattern as titling.

B.5 Controls checklist (required outcomes)

Stated as outcomes the build must achieve; the concrete mechanisms (isolation technique, password hashing, session & token handling, connection pooling) are proposed in the Functional Specification Proposal.

Tenant isolation — the #1 risk

  • Isolation enforced at the data layer, not only in application code, so a forgotten check cannot leak data.
  • The application's normal data access cannot bypass the isolation boundary.
  • Writes are constrained to the acting tenant (no insert / update into another tenant).
  • Tenant context is derived from the authenticated session/token server-side — never from a client-supplied identifier (defends OWASP API #1, broken object-level authorization).
  • A CI negative test proves tenant A cannot read, update, delete or insert-as tenant B — at both API and data layers; any leak fails the build.

Authentication & authorization

  • Hardened sessions (theft-resistant, rotated on login, idle + absolute timeouts, server-side invalidation).
  • Passwords stored with a strong, salted, memory-hard hash; MFA available and required for sensitive roles.
  • Login throttling / lockout; safe password reset (single-use, time-limited, no account enumeration).
  • API / portal tokens short-lived, scoped to the role, and fully validated.
  • Cross-site request forgery defended for browser sessions.
  • Role-based authorization with field-level masking of PII / title / payment data.

Cross-cutting

  • Secrets held in a managed secret store, never in source control; keys rotated.
  • Encryption in transit and at rest; payment instruments and sensitive PII additionally tokenized / encrypted.
  • Immutable, append-only audit log (the non-repudiation control).
  • Rate limiting per IP / token / tenant on authentication, API and upload.
  • Server-side input validation; no injection-prone query construction.
  • Secure file upload: real content-type verification, randomized names, private storage, malware scan, image re-encode.
  • Server-side URL fetches restricted to prevent server-side request forgery.

B.6 Data-model requirements

To satisfy the above, the data model must additionally carry: an immutable audit log (append-only, tenant-scoped); e-signature consent records (consent + document hash + signer / timestamp / address); customer residency (to drive per-state breach notice); a retention policy (per-record-type disposal floors versus the 2-year default); and certificate-of-insurance records with expiry dates (the check-out gate). Payment instruments store only a token + last4 + brand, never full numbers.

C.2 Import pipeline specification

Column mapping

  • Fuzzy auto-map to canonical fields (target ~95% auto-matched), with manual remap.
  • Show, per canonical field, an inline description + sample value; allow split/merge (one “Customer Name” → first/last).
  • Persist the mapping profile keyed to the dealer’s source DMS so repeat uploads are seamless.

Validation — three layers

  • Per-column: type, required, enum. Normalize messy legacy values (“sold”, “SOLD”, “S” → canonical unit_status/deal_stage) via a mapping table; infer date formats (MM/DD vs DD/MM).
  • Referential: import in dependency order (units + customers before deals); reject a deal whose unit/customer key doesn’t resolve.
  • De-duplication: units by VIN / serial / stock_no, customers by email / phone — surface collisions, never silently merge.

Preview, commit, errors, re-runs

  • Errors vs warnings split — the partial-success mechanism (warnings commit; errors hold).
  • Row-level inline errors with a “show only problem rows” filter, in-app fix, undo; downloadable annotated error file for re-upload.
  • Failed rows → staging queue, never abort the whole file.
  • Idempotent re-runs: every row keyed on a stable external_ref; the bulk API accepts an Idempotency-Key.
  • Robust parsing: BOM/encoding detection, Excel-CSV quirks, embedded commas/newlines; async background worker with an import_id; chunk 50–500 rows (MVP may cap row count).

C.3 Migration strategy

  • Split static/master (units, customers — migrate days ahead) from dynamic/transactional (open deals/WIP — migrate at cutover).
  • Advise dealers to “drain the line” (~2 weeks) so open WIP is minimal at go-live.
  • Migrate active data only; archive closed history as CSV (not live records).
  • Reconciliation screen before go-live: source-vs-target counts per entity + a sampled spot-check (e.g. 10 units, 5 customers).
  • Rollback triggers: a hard-stop time and a > 0.5% record-count variance auto-revert; keep the old DMS read-accessible through cutover.
  • One-time migration is the default; live two-way sync with a legacy DMS is out of scope. Offer a no-commit dry-run.

Appendix D · Derived-Field Formulas

Unambiguous formula definitions so two independent rebuilds compute identical numbers. The single biggest source of divergence is the age clock; it is resolved by persisting separate date stamps per unit and naming which clock each metric uses.

D.0 Conventions (state once, apply everywhere)

  • One day-count convention (inclusive vs exclusive of endpoints), applied uniformly to age, days-to-sell, recon, DSO and aging buckets.

  • One global “sold” trigger — booked / funded / delivered; default delivered (ASC 606). It propagates to units-sold, velocity, conversion and revenue recognition.
  • Persist five date clocks per unit: acquired_at, recon_started_at, frontline_ready_at, sold_at (booked), delivered_at.

Appendix F · Accounting-System Export

The bookkeeper must be able to manually export financial transactions to the dealer’s accounting system on demand — QuickBooks first (both Online and Desktop), with other systems supported later without a rewrite.

F.1 Requirements

  • Manual, on-demand export of financial transactions — invoices, payments and summary journal entries — to the dealer's accounting system.
  • Must support QuickBooks first (both QuickBooks Online and QuickBooks Desktop), and be extensible to other systems (Xero, Sage, NetSuite, …) without re-engineering the export core.
  • A one-time chart-of-accounts mapping per dealer (the system's revenue / COGS / AR / sales-tax / fees / deposits → the dealer's own accounts).
  • No duplicate postings on re-export — each exported batch is idempotent and marked as exported.
  • The baseline export must work without granting the system third-party access it cannot govern and without depending on an external approval gate.
  • Rental money is a distinct stream and must export as one. Rental (operating-lease) revenue is recognized straight-line over the term — never through the sale pipeline; the rented unit stays on the books and depreciates. Required GL buckets beyond the sale set: Rental Revenue, Fleet Depreciation, Fleet Maintenance, Customer Deposits (liability), Deferred/Accrued Rent (when billing ≠ recognition). Variable charges (mileage overage, damage) recognize when incurred. Security deposits are liabilities until applied (itemized) or refunded — never revenue. An RPO that becomes “reasonably certain” to exercise converts the contract to a sales-type event (the proposed mechanics live in the Functional Specification Proposal). Sales tax applies per recurring rental invoice and is jurisdiction- and duration-dependent — configurable, like all tax.

The format choices (file vs API), the QuickBooks-specific constraints, and the canonical-model + per-target-adapter design that keeps "other systems later" cheap are analyzed and recommended in the Functional Specification Proposal.
